PART A

What NLP tasks are, and how they are scored

Natural language processing (NLP) means computing with text. The lecture sorts every task into three buckets.

A label is the answer you want, for example "positive". A task is the kind of question asked of the text. The bucket matters because it decides how you check the model.

PART B

Tokenization: cutting text into pieces

A model only takes numbers. Step one is to cut text into units called tokens. Each token then gets a number, and later a vector.

A vocabulary is the list of every token the model knows. If a word was never seen during training, the model has no entry for it. It becomes "unknown", called OOV (out of vocabulary).

Length, then and now

Smaller pieces mean fewer unknown words and a longer sequence. Attention then compares every token with every other, so cost grows with the square of the length.

Two fixes shipped. FlashAttention does that same math in fast memory. Sliding-window attention lets a token see a neighborhood, and depth widens the view. Context went from about 512–2,048 tokens to 128,000 as a normal frontier length, and past a million on some models.

PART C

Embeddings: giving tokens meaningful vectors

A token index has no meaning. The goal is an arrow for each token, with related meanings pointing the same way. One-hot fails at that. Word2vec was the 2013 way to learn the arrows. A current model still starts with a learned lookup, one row per token, and every later layer rewrites the arrow with attention, so the same spelling in two sentences is no longer the same vector. Search models are a separate case: a transformer encoder trained to pack a whole sentence into one vector.

The word vector you keep is the hidden layer: one-hot × W₁. A one-hot has a single 1, so this multiplication just picks one row of W₁. Training is what makes that row meaningful.

How wide should the vector be?

  • Wider holds more and costs more. That trade has not changed.
  • 768 is the width of a small 2018 model (BERT-base) and is still a sensible teaching size.
  • Current language models are several thousand wide, and the largest are wider, with many more layers. People start from a published width and scale it.

When is training done?

  • Track the proxy loss against epochs (full passes over the data). Stop when it stops falling.
  • Then judge the vectors on the real downstream task. The proxy loss is not the goal.

Learned vectors carry relations: king → queen, and Paris is to France as Berlin is to Germany.

A word2vec arrow belongs to the spelling. "Bank" is the same arrow in "river bank" and in "rob a bank". Attention, next, rewrites the arrow from the sentence around it. That contextual arrow is what current models actually use after the first layer.
PART D

Reading in order: RNNs and LSTMs

Averaging token arrows throws away order. A recurrent net reads one token at a time and rewrites a notepad. That was the sequence model from the 1980s through the mid-2010s. Current chat models do not read this way. They let each token look at earlier tokens with attention. Around 2023 a running state came back as state-space models (Mamba), whose cost grows in a straight line with length. Hybrids mix that state with attention. They are a side path. They have not replaced the transformer in chat.

The hidden state (also called activation or context vector, written h or A) is like a notepad. After each word, the network rewrites the notepad using what was there plus the new word. Whatever is not on the notepad is lost.

Three problems with recurrence

1 · It forgets

The whole sentence must fit in one state vector, so distant words fade. Long-range dependencies are weak.

2 · Gradients vanish or explode

The last prediction depends on every earlier state. Back-propagation through time multiplies a long chain of factors.

3 · It is slow

Token 50 needs states 1 to 49 first. It cannot run in parallel, so long sequences mean long training steps.

PART E

Attention: a direct link to every token

Instead of hoping a notepad still holds a distant word, each word looks straight at the others and mixes in more of the ones that matter. The 2014 version added that look onto a recurrent reader. The 2017 version dropped the reader and made the look the whole step. The formula below is still the math in current models. Around it, three fittings changed: FlashAttention computes it without storing the giant score grid, rotary position folds distance into the query and the key, and keys and values are shared across groups of heads so the cache of past tokens stays smaller.

Attention (2014)

While a decoder picks the next word, it may look directly at the relevant part of the source sentence. It no longer relies only on the compressed state.

Self-attention (2017 transformer)

No token-by-token reading at all. To build one token's vector, the model looks at every token in the sequence at once. The vector now depends on context, so "bank" by a river and "bank" in a robbery get different vectors.

Query, key, value: the library analogy

Think of a library. The query is what you are looking for. Each book has a key, its label on the spine. Each book also has a value, its contents. You compare your query with every key. The better a key matches, the more of that book's value goes into your answer. In a transformer all three are learned projections of the token vectors. They are not fixed tables.

PART F

The transformer for translation

The diagram is the 2017 translation machine: an encoder reads the English, a decoder writes the French one token at a time. Click a block for that original wiring. In 2018 the block split. BERT kept only the encoder and classifies. GPT kept only the decoder and predicts the next word. T5 kept both stacks for text-to-text. From GPT-3 on, scale went into the decoder, because next-word prediction matches chat. Current large language models are that decoder-only stack. The encoder still embeds or classifies text. Cross-attention comes back when a second stream must be read, such as image tokens, though many vision models place those tokens in the same decoder sequence.

Why a position encoding is added

Self-attention has no order until position is added. The 2017 way adds a sine-and-cosine vector to the token. A learned table of positions does the same job and has no vector past the longest training length.

Relative schemes came next. T5 adds a learned bias for the distance between two tokens. ALiBi adds a fixed penalty that grows with distance. Rotary embeddings (RoPE, 2021) are the usual choice now. They rotate the query and the key so the score depends on how far apart the tokens are. Longer context is a stretch of that rotation (position interpolation, YaRN, and similar), not a new table.

The feed-forward is still wider than the residual stream, so it has room to build features. The activation moved from ReLU to GELU to SwiGLU, a gated unit. That gated wide layer is the usual feed-forward in current models. Some models replace it with a mixture of experts: several feed-forwards and a gate, and each token uses only a few of them.

Keeping the model from being over-sure

After one prefix, more than one next word can be right: "what a great day", "what a great book". A target of probability 1 on a single word is too sharp. Label smoothing was the translation-era fix: move a little probability onto the other words. It tended to raise BLEU.

Chat training took another road, and that is the road in use now. Pretraining still predicts the next token on a huge corpus. Supervised fine-tuning then shows the kind of answer you want. Preference training says this answer over that one, first with a reward model on human rankings (RLHF), later also with direct preference methods that skip a separate reward model. Reasoning training samples several attempts, keeps the ones a checker accepts, and raises the chance of that kind of attempt. Softening a one-hot label is no longer how models are kept from being over-sure. The slider below is the old fix, so the arithmetic is visible.

Questions that are easy to miss

How do I pick the vector width?
It depends on the downstream task and on how much latency and cost you can afford. Tasks with many outcomes want wider vectors, and simple tasks can use smaller ones. Hundreds to thousands is normal, and 768 is a common pick. Published widths are a reasonable starting point.
Why is the toy vocabulary size v = 6?
It is a toy size. A word-level cut makes v large because every inflection is its own token. At inference, any unseen token shares one reserved unknown vector. Word cuts hit that often, subword cuts less often, and character cuts avoid it.
In cross-attention, is the decoder arrow a query, key, or value?
The query. It asks: given what I am about to say, which source tokens matter? Keys and values come from the encoder.
Why divide by √d_k?
As the query and key width grows, dot products grow larger. Dividing by the square root of the key width keeps them in range. You can see the effect in the toy attention widget above.
Could every head learn the same thing?
Nothing forbids it. Gradient descent on next-word prediction has no incentive to copy one map, and in practice heads converge to different representations.
Why both cosine similarity and the raw dot product? LEFT OPEN
Cosine is the normalized dot product, and the raw dot product is also used. People usually care about the angle. Whether vector length means anything depends on how the vectors were trained. The lecture called cosine "a measure, not a perfect one" and gave no stronger rule.

Check yourself

1 · A sentiment set is 99% positive. Why does always predicting positive look good under accuracy, and what does F1 combine?
Accuracy counts the share of all predictions that are right, so 99 of 100 are right even though the model never finds a negative review. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Scored on the rare class, it is 0 here because recall is 0.
2 · Why are one-hot vectors all orthogonal, and what do CBOW and skip-gram each predict?
Each one-hot vector has its single 1 in a different coordinate, so the dot product of any two different tokens is 0. Cosine 0 means orthogonal. CBOW predicts the target word from its surrounding words. Skip-gram predicts the surrounding words from the target word.
3 · What may masked self-attention see, where do cross-attention's Q, K, V come from, and why add position encoding?
Masked self-attention sees only the current target token and the ones already produced, never future ones. In cross-attention the query comes from the decoder, and the keys and values come from the encoder's final outputs. Position encoding is added because attention links all tokens directly and would otherwise have no sense of order.
4 · Why does a subword tokenizer lower OOV risk but cost more?
It shares roots ("bear" in "bears"), so fewer words are completely unknown. Cutting into smaller pieces makes sequences longer, and model cost grows with sequence length.
5 · Name two reasons the transformer replaced RNNs.
Any two of these three. First, direct links let it use distant tokens without squeezing everything into one state, which fixes forgetting. Second, gradients do not pass through a long chain of per-step factors. Third, the whole sequence is processed as matrix multiplies in parallel, which suits GPUs, instead of one token after another.

PART A

Where a token sits

A link from every token to every other token does not know which token came first. Order has to be put back.

A position method is the extra signal that says “this token is the 4th, that one is the 20th,” or “these two are 16 steps apart.” An absolute method names the index. A relative method names the gap.

In the sentence “a cute teddy bear is reading,” an absolute method treats position 1 as the token “a” plus a position-1 vector of the same width as the token vector, because the two are added. Swap “cute” and “bear” and the set of links is unchanged unless that signal changes with them.

The fixed wave, and why equal positions match best

The first absolute methods add one vector per index at the input, once.

  • Learned table. Gradient descent fits one vector per index, from position 1 through the training maximum. The sketch of that table stops at 512. The table soaks up whatever the training texts do at that index, and an index past the maximum has no vector.
  • Fixed sinusoid. Nothing is learned. Dimension pair i uses a speed ωi = 10000−2i / d, and position m stores sin(ωi m) and cos(ωi m). Any length has a value, including lengths the model never trained on. Quality matched the learned table.
dot(position m, position n) = Σi cos(ωi (m − n))

The cosine-difference identity turns the dot product into a sum that depends only on the gap m − n. Cosine similarity is that dot product scaled by the two lengths, so closeness of two position vectors is a function of relative distance. cos 0 = 1, so every term is at its peak when m = n, and the match is largest there. Cosine is periodic, so past π the match is not a strict slide downward: farther is not always smaller. A low index i has a large ωi and a fast wave. A high index i has a small ωi and a slow wave. A sketch of about 50 positions shows that pattern, fast waves in the early dimensions and slow waves in the later ones.

The wave was a way to get a relative gap out of an absolute vector, and to have a value ready for every index. The models that followed wanted that same “nearby ranks higher than far” pressure, and they put it inside the attention score instead of in a vector added at the input.

Rotary position, the usual method

The attention mix is softmax(Q Kᵀ / √dk) V. A number added before the softmax is renormalized with everything else, so the weights still add to 1. Far pairs can be given a more negative score than near pairs, and the mix stays a valid mix.

  • T5 relative bias. Distances m − n are put in buckets. Each bucket is a learned scalar inside the softmax.
  • ALiBi (attention with linear biases), from the train-short, test-long line of work, replaces that learned scalar with a fixed function of the raw gap: a slope for each head, times how far apart the two positions are. Nothing about the bias is learned.
  • RoPE (rotary position embedding) does not add a bias. It rotates the query by an angle tied to its position m and the key by an angle tied to its position n. Their match then contains a rotation whose angle depends on n − m.
In a plane, a vector of length R at angle φ is R (cos φ, sin φ). Multiply by the turn [cos θ, −sin θ; sin θ, cos θ] and the length stays R while the angle becomes φ + θ. RoPE is that turn, with θ set by the token’s index.

For a width d greater than 2, the same 2×2 turn is applied to pairs of coordinates. There are d/2 pairs. Pair i, with i running from 1 to d/2, uses a fixed angle θi that depends on i and on d. It is the same family of speeds as ωi in the sinusoid. The query and the key have to share that width, so the turn is sized to the vector being matched, not glued on as an extra input. The RoFormer appendix upper-bounds the query–key weight. The bound shrinks as |n − m| grows, and it oscillates, so the long-range decay is real and is not a smooth curve drawn through an experiment.

A learned absolute table overfits the indexes it saw, and it has a hole past the last row. ALiBi’s formula is only the raw gap, which is a tight family. RoPE keeps a relative gap inside every layer’s match, and the turn does not change the vector’s length.

On 31 October 2026, chat models use RoPE. A model is trained at a base length. Position interpolation squeezes a longer index back into the angles seen in training. YaRN does that squeeze differently for the fast waves and the slow waves. Both are ways to run RoPE past the training length. They have not replaced RoPE. A context of 128,000 tokens is a normal frontier length, and some models go past a million. ALiBi, a learned absolute table, and the T5 bucket bias still appear on some models. They have not replaced RoPE on the chat default.

PART B

Keeping the numbers in a tight range

Each block wraps a sublayer, attention or the feed-forward, in a residual add and a scale. The scale pulls extreme components back so the next layer can be fit.

Internal covariate shift is the name for activations whose statistics jump from layer to layer. A residual adds the block’s input back onto the sublayer’s output, so the layer can learn a change instead of a whole new vector. A norm rescales that vector.

Where the norm sits

The 2017 block normalizes after the add. The form used to train deep stacks normalizes first, runs the sublayer, then adds the untouched input. The residual path can carry the signal through even when the sublayer is still wild.

Original pass: post-norm

Norm( x + Sublayer(x) )

Add the residual, then scale the sum. Layer norm sat on that sum.

Usual pass: pre-norm

x + Sublayer( Norm(x) )

Scale first, run attention or the feed-forward, then add x. This is the pass in current chat models.

Layer norm, RMSNorm, and the batch axis

Layer norm looks across the components of one token. It subtracts their mean, divides by their standard deviation, then applies a learned scale γ and a learned shift β. A tiny constant inside the square root keeps a zero vector from dividing by zero.

LayerNorm(x) = γ · (x − mean) / √(variance + ε) + β

RMSNorm (root-mean-square normalization) divides x by the root mean square of its components and learns only γ. There is no mean and no shift β. Convergence is comparable, with fewer parameters, so the step is cheaper. On 31 October 2026 the usual pair is pre-norm with RMSNorm.

RMSNorm(x) = γ · x / √( mean(x²) + ε )

Batch norm normalizes each coordinate across the batch. The statistic depends on the other rows, so a training batch and a single row at inference can disagree. These models stayed with a norm computed inside one token’s vector. RMSNorm replaced layer norm on that same per-token axis. It did not replace the decision to leave the batch axis alone. Batch norm remains a side path here. It has not become the usual norm in this stack.

PART C

The cost of every token looking at every token

Full self-attention is a dense n × n pattern. The work grows as O(n²) in the sequence length n.

n is how many tokens are in the pass. If every token scores every token, there are n × n scores. Double the prompt and the grid is four times as wide.

Exact attention, and the usual way to run it

The original pattern scores every pair. That is still the math inside the context a chat model claims. FlashAttention computes those same scores in fast on-chip GPU memory and does not write the full matrix out to slow memory. The softmax is the exact one. On 31 October 2026 this is the usual way to run full attention. It does not drop far pairs. A shorter prompt weakens the reason to avoid the full pattern, because n² is still modest when n is modest.

The grid is expensive to store, not only to multiply. Doing the exact mix in fast memory keeps the definition and spends less traffic.

A neighborhood instead of the full grid

Longformer (2020) keeps a neighborhood around each token and drops the rest. The usual name now is sliding-window attention. Implementations tile the scores so the full matrix is never built. A token sees its window. Those neighbors see the next window. Depth grows an effective field the way a stack of convolutions grows a receptive field.

Sketched windows are tiny. With a long context the window is on the order of several thousand tokens. Some layers can be local and some global, interleaved, and there is no single recipe for that mix. The design shown on a Mistral model puts a window at every layer. On 31 October 2026 the window is a side path for a long prompt. It has not replaced exact attention. A 128,000-token context is a normal frontier length, and some models go past a million; the usual math on that span is still the full mix, run with a kernel such as FlashAttention, unless the model deliberately windows.

Every layer uses a window, and the fact you need sits farther away than depth times the window. No layer ever sees it.

PART D

Sharing keys and values, and the KV cache

Multi-head attention gives each head its own query, key, and value. The heads run in parallel, their outputs are concatenated, and one output projection mixes them. The change here shares keys and values and leaves queries unshared.

A head is one search. The query is what this token is looking for. The key is the label each token offers. The value is the content that gets mixed in when the label matches. A KV cache is the stored keys and values of tokens already written, so the next step does not recompute them.

In a sentence about a law and its application, the token “its” put high weight on “law” and on “application,” the two antecedents. Separate heads peaked on different tokens. Queries stay per head because each head is allowed to test a different kind of match. Keys and values are the part that gets reread.

What the cache holds while the model writes

Decoding attends the new token to every earlier token. The cache stores those earlier keys and values. The new token still builds its own query, key, and value, and the new key and value are appended. Past queries are not kept: the new token does not need them. Sharing keys and values across heads is what keeps that store smaller.

The same sharing can sit in any attention block, including cross-attention. A decoder-only model has dropped the encoder, so the sharing sits on masked self-attention. Training does not need the cache: the whole sequence is run at once, with the future hidden. The cache is the writing-time form of the same math.

The search should stay varied. The memory of the past should stay small. Grouped-query attention is the point between “a private key for every head” and “one key for all heads.”

Which one you ship depends on quality, latency, model size, compute, and input length. On 31 October 2026 grouped-query attention is the usual compromise. Multi-query was the extreme cut. Full multi-head remains on smaller models. Not every model uses grouped-query attention.

The cache is dropped and every new token recomputes keys and values for the whole prefix. The answers can match. The time and the memory traffic do not.

PART F

BERT: a bidirectional encoder, and how it was trained

BERT is Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. The encoder’s self-attention lets every token see every other token. A causal mask does the opposite: a token sees itself and the past only. A decoder such as GPT is that causal case, so those encodings are not bidirectional.

CLS is a token stuck on the front. After the stack, its vector has mixed the whole sequence, and a classifier reads that vector. SEP marks the boundary between two sentences. PAD fills a batch out to a fixed width. Masked language modeling hides some tokens and asks for them back. Next-sentence prediction asks whether sentence B really followed sentence A.

ELMo, the same year, built bidirectional word vectors with a stacked bidirectional LSTM. The vectors were contextual, which is why a new classifier can be a small head on a frozen or lightly updated encoder. Recurrence made ELMo hard to scale. The transformer encoder is the line that scaled. ELMo has not remained the usual way to build those vectors.

The two training jobs, and the tokens that make them possible

Training is two-stage. Pre-training uses unlabeled text. Fine-tuning keeps those weights and adds a small network, typically a linear map, for the target task. Little labeled data is required for the second stage. Generation is unavailable without a decoder. Two stages are heavier than a one-shot classical pipeline.

  • Masked language modeling learns local structure. It needs no external labels: the label is the hidden token. Picked positions are replaced at random. 80% become the mask token, 10% stay unchanged (the target is still that token), and 10% become a random word. Predicting the token forces the use of left and right context. The 10% that stay visible stop the model from treating “not masked” as “already correct.”
  • Next-sentence prediction learns whether sentence order is real. It is classification, not generation. Two sentences are packed. A head on CLS says whether they were consecutive. Half the time the order is the true document order. Half the time it is shuffled. The label comes from that document order.

The input vector is the sum of three pieces: a token embedding from a lookup table, an absolute position vector, and a segment vector. BERT’s position vectors are learned, one per index, out to the context limit of 512. Segment encodings are two learned vectors, A on every token of the first sentence and B on every token of the second, fit so the next-sentence job can tell the sentences apart. A single-sentence pass uses one segment id for the whole sentence. The encoder block itself is unchanged: self-attention, then a feed-forward. The two losses are what shape the projections for downstream classification.

WordPiece learns merge rules that maximize likelihood on the training text and builds a vocabulary on the order of 30,000 in this paper. Other vocabularies in this family sit around 104 to 105. ByT5’s byte vocabulary of 256 is the exception. Checkpoints are also split by cased and uncased preprocessing. BERT base is 110 million parameters, context 512. In the paper, depth is written L, width H, and head count A. They are the same three sizes.

PART A

What counts as a large language model

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence by predicting one next token at a time. The probability of a whole string is the product of those steps, which is the same fact as a sum of log probabilities, starting from the beginning-of-sequence token.

A token is one piece from the vocabulary. The model does not emit a word from a dictionary of meanings. It emits a distribution over piece ids, and a rule turns that distribution into the next id.

Large in three ways at once

The name was loose around 2018–19, and early uses sometimes included an encoder that only builds vectors. The definition used here keeps models that write text, and that are large in parameter count, pretraining tokens, and compute together. An encoder-only model is excluded because it does not produce text. What remains is text-to-text, large in those three ways, and decoder-only.

PART B

Mixture of experts

A mixture of experts does not fire every parameter on every token. There are n expert networks. A gate, also called a router, maps the token’s vector x to a distribution over experts. The output is the sum of the expert outputs, weighted by that gate. The gate and the experts are trained together: forward pass, loss, backprop.

Picture a room with a mathematician, a physicist, a chemist, and a historian. A math question does not have to wake all four at full volume. An expert is one feed-forward network. The gate is a small scorer that decides which of those networks answer. A model with one feed-forward and no gate has no mixture.

Dense gate, sparse gate, and where it sits

A dense mixture does not cap how many experts take part. The gate weights lie between 0 and 1 and simply favor some experts, and everyone can still contribute. A sparse mixture keeps only the top k. k is a chosen hyperparameter. k = 1 was the typical early setting. k = 2 is the other setting this design was worked out with. The output is then the sum, over the chosen experts only, of gate(x) times expert(x). Sparse routing is what cuts floating-point work relative to a dense mixture.

The experts replace the feed-forward, not attention and not the norm. That block is the expensive one: a vector of width d is lifted to a wider width and projected back. The original two-matrix form has on the order of 2 × d × dff parameters, plus bias. The usual expert is a SwiGLU, the gated form of that lift, so the slot holds three matrices. Attention’s projections are the smaller part.

Each token is routed from its own representation, after masked self-attention has made that representation contextual. Only the chosen experts are evaluated. There is one gate per layer, not one per head. The heads are concatenated and projected back to width d before the gate. Gates are not shared across blocks, so layer 1 can pick a different expert than layer 2. Expert count is independent of head count.

Each attention head is given its own pool of experts. The gate never sees a per-head vector. It sees the joined vector, once per layer.

Routing collapse, and the extra loss

The failure mode is routing collapse: the gate keeps picking one or two experts, and the rest stay inactive. The fix is an extra loss. A coefficient α times the number of experts times a sum over experts of a term built from two quantities:

  • f(i) is the fraction of tokens in the batch actually sent to expert i.
  • P(i) is the average gate probability for expert i on that batch.
extra loss = α · N · Σi f(i) · P(i)

The gate is a projection into N dimensions followed by a softmax, so the probabilities for one token sum to 1, and P(i) is their average. The product f(i) P(i) is large when one expert both receives the tokens and already owns the probability. The sum is smallest when every expert is used evenly. That is the push.

P(i) is an average of softmax outputs, so it has an ordinary gradient. STILL OPEN f(i) is a count of hard assignments. How that count itself is differentiated was left unresolved; automatic differentiation through a relaxation is the usual hope, and the gradient is not derived here. Noisy gating adds noise to the gate scores so a different expert is sometimes tried. It is an extra push. It has not replaced the balancing loss.

Capacity you never route to is capacity you paid to store and did not train. The extra term exists so the gate keeps using the experts it has.
PART C

Choosing the next token

Once the model is trained, the next token is chosen from a distribution over the vocabulary. All of the procedures below are inference. Training uses teacher forcing and runs the whole sequence at once. Nothing inside the stack is random. Only the draw of the next token is.

The stack ends in a list of scores, one per vocabulary token: a linear map from the model width to vocabulary size V. A softmax turns scores into probabilities that add to 1. Temperature T divides the scores before that softmax. The outcomes are discrete tokens. They do not sit on a line, so this is not a bell curve of “nearby words.”

Greedy, beam, and the sampling pass used for chat

Low T spikes the distribution on the largest score. High T flattens it toward uniform, which is the setting used for more varied text. As T goes to 0 the mass sits on the max score. As T goes to infinity every token gets probability 1/V. A strictly positive T makes repeated runs differ. Setting T to 0 is the theoretical way to force a single output. STILL OPEN even a zero-temperature or greedy run can change across runs, because hardware adds values on very different scales in an order that is not fixed. The math is a single winner. The addition order is not part of that math.

Greedy takes the single highest probability. The forward pass is deterministic, so the same input yields the same string, and the string is not diverse. It is only locally optimal: the best token now need not lie on the highest-probability full sequence.

TOY NUMBERS One next token has probability 0.8 and another 0.2. They sum to 1. Taking 0.8 can still lose if every later token on that branch is unlikely and the 0.2 branch is confident later.

Beam search keeps the k best partial sequences. k is the beam width. At the end it picks the sequence with the highest score. The score is the sum of the log probabilities of the successive tokens. Each probability is below 1, so a longer product shrinks, and the raw score favors short strings. A length correction, on the order of 1 over the number of tokens raised to a power, puts the longer string back on a fair footing. The power is a setting. Beam search is costly, it still hunts a single likely string, and it is the typical habit for machine translation rather than for open answers.

Sampling draws the next token from the distribution, so a low-probability token remains possible. From the prefix beginning-of-sequence, “A”, “teddy”, “bear”, the high-probability continuations in the worked picture are fluffy, gentle, kind, and smart. Airplane is unlikely and is not impossible. Top-k keeps the k highest tokens and draws only among them. The worked cutoff is k = 4, which keeps those four and drops airplane. Top-p (nucleus) sorts tokens and keeps the shortest prefix whose probabilities add up to at least p, then renormalizes and draws. Top-p can drop a token that top-k would have kept, if the larger ones already cover p.

The usual chat pass on 31 October 2026 is the sampling pass: divide by a moderate temperature, cut the tail, draw, append, and repeat until the end-of-sequence token. Low temperature or greedy is the usual pass when the answer should be stable, as in code or a short exact fact. Beam search remains the translation habit. It has not become the chat default.

Context rot is the failure where a fact that was easy to recover in a short text gets buried as the text grows. For a lookup, the incentive is to show the relevant slice.

The window is a budget the attention mix spends. Filling it with extra text spends the budget on tokens that are not the question.
PART E

Changing the answer without changing the weights

In-context learning changes behavior with no update to the parameters. “Learning” is a loose word. Nothing about the weights is learned. The new information sits in the window.

Zero-shot and few-shot

Zero-shot sends the query and no examples. Few-shot prepends input–output examples. A worked bedtime story names one bear Teddy, shows the story that was wanted, then asks for a story about a bear named Bob. Examples usually steer the model. They cost tokens and compute. A finite set of examples also pins the model to those examples and can hurt cases off that set.

A strong model can match or beat few-shot when the instruction itself explains how to reason. On 31 October 2026 a clear instruction with no examples is enough for many jobs. Few-shot is still used when the format is odd or the task is narrow. It has not been retired, and it is not required on every prompt.

A chain of thought, and a plan

Chain of thought writes a rationale before the answer. On “how old is this teddy bear?”, a bare number skips the link. A written chain shows it. The chain is also a debug trace. Ask “how old will the bear be next year?” and a chain that answers as if the year were 2019 means the context carried the wrong date. You see that without inspecting weights. The cost is more generated tokens and more time.

Plan-and-solve asks for a plan and then for the plan to be carried out. It is a prompting pattern. No score is attached to it here. It has not become a separate architecture.

The usual reply is still one pass of next-token choices. For a hard question, asking for the rationale is a common prompt. Some models are trained to spend a run of tokens on that rationale before the final answer, so the chain is a habit of the model rather than a line you must type. That trained habit is the usual frontier setting for a hard question. The typed request is what you still have when the model will not think unless asked. Neither has cleared the other off the table: easy questions still get a direct answer.

The weights are a bad place to look for a wrong year. The chain is text, so a wrong step points at a wrong line in the prompt.

Self-consistency

Self-consistency samples several full responses, strips them down to the answer, and takes a majority vote. The samples are parallel and do not enter one another’s context, so the wait is about the slowest sample, not the sum of the samples. Knowing that this helped requires a benchmark with ground-truth labels. The comparisons this rests on are arithmetic and other mathematical tasks. No score is given here. The answer is pulled out by an instruction to put it last, by a regular expression, or by another model.

On 31 October 2026 this is a side path for a question with one checkable answer. It has not replaced a single sample as the default chat reply.

PART F

Making each new token cheaper

A saving is either exact, the same math with less waste, or a change to the math that still aims at a good answer. Some “savings” are really a different model: fewer key-value groups, or a compressed cache.

The KV cache, and why grouped-query attention shrinks it

During writing, past keys and values are stored and reused. The current token still builds its own key and value. Past queries are not kept. Training does not use this cache, because teacher forcing runs every position together. Grouped-query attention shrinks how many key and value vectors exist. With h heads, h key-value groups is ordinary multi-head attention, and 1 group is multi-query attention. Grouped-query attention sits between them. There is no single group count that every model uses. On 31 October 2026 grouped-query attention is the usual compromise, multi-query is the extreme, and full multi-head remains on smaller models.

The new token must look up the past. It does not need the past’s searches. Storing keys and values, and sharing them across heads, is the exact memory cut for that lookup.

Pages of cache, and a compressed cache

PagedAttention, the scheme behind the inference package vLLM, stops a server from reserving a full maximum context for every request up front. Decoding ends at an end-of-sequence token, so a naive reservation wastes memory. Internal fragmentation is space reserved and still empty. External fragmentation is gaps between blocks that are too small to reuse. The scheme uses fixed blocks — 16 tokens in the design that introduced it — plus a map from token positions to those blocks. It cuts fragmentation by a lot. No ratio is stated here. The values in the cache do not change. Only the packing changes, so the attention math stays the same.

TOY SKETCH A maximum context of 2,000 tokens, reserved twice for two requests, fills memory before more requests fit. The 2,000 is a small sketch of the waste. A real frontier window of 128,000 or more makes reserving the maximum for every request more expensive, not less.

Multi-head latent attention compresses the key projection through a lower-dimensional vector, then decompresses. The heard shortening “multi-latent attention” is this same idea, not a second method. The compression can be shared by keys and values and across heads, so the cache stores one vector per token per block, while separate decompression matrices can still make a key differ from a value. The compressed width is a fixed choice. One shared width is the setup this design was built with; different widths would be possible. The sharing was credited with a regularization benefit. No metric is given here. DeepSeek-V2 is the model this compression was tied to. On 31 October 2026 it is a side path used by models in that line. It has not replaced grouped-query attention as the usual cache.

A draft of several tokens, then a check

Speculative decoding lets a smaller draft model propose several next tokens. The large target scores those tokens in one forward pass. Each draft token is kept when the target likes it at least as much as the draft did. When the target likes it less, it is kept only some of the time, in proportion to the two probabilities, and otherwise a replacement is drawn from what remains so the overall draw still follows the target. One way to write the keep test is: draw u uniformly between 0 and 1, and accept if u is less than the minimum of 1 and ptarget / pdraft. If the draft is accepted, one target pass advances many tokens. If it fails, generation resumes from the last good token with that adjusted distribution. The token after the draft comes for free from the same pass: the target already computed the next distribution.

TOY SKETCH On “my teddy bear,” the draft proposes “is,” then “cute” and “smart,” faster because the draft is small. The target scores those tokens together.

A wide pass is cheap because inference is memory-bound. Compute is not the limiter, so one pass over several tokens costs about as much as one token. On 31 October 2026 speculative decoding is a common way to serve a model. It does not change the model you trained. The draws are meant to follow that model’s distribution.

Multi-token prediction keeps the draft inside the same model. Extra heads sit on the last decoder state. Training predicts several future tokens, not only the next one. At test time every extra head is a draft and the first head is the main model. Draft tokens are fed back to that first head and accepted or rejected. The check in that line of work is greedy, not the speculative-decoding test, because the changed objective no longer guarantees the original next-token distribution. Two points to keep: the new objective, and the fact that draft and target are one model. Next-token prediction is still the usual training loss. Multi-token prediction is a side path. It has not replaced that loss. Adam remains the usual optimizer for either objective. STILL OPEN Muon is a live candidate and is not the established default.

PART A

Next-token pretraining

This is the expensive stage in compute and in money. In more than 90 percent of cases the model is decoder-only: read a prefix, predict the next token, and repeat. The loss is text to text.

A token is one piece of text, from the earlier session. Pretraining shows the model a real document and, at every position, asks which token comes next. A parameter is one stored number inside the model that this run is allowed to change. Teacher forcing means the prefix is the true document, not the model’s own earlier guesses. At generation time the model has to live with its own guesses. Training and generation are different in that one place.

The loss

loss = − log P(true next token | tokens so far)

The model assigns a probability to every token in the vocabulary. The loss pulls probability onto the token that actually came next. Summed over a document, that is the whole pretraining objective. There is no label such as “spam” or “positive” in this stage.

Shared text understanding is in the weights after this run, because every task later will need it. Training a fresh model for each task would relearn that shared part from scratch.

The corpus, then and on 31 Oct 2026

Model collapse is the failure mode in which a new model is trained on text that earlier models wrote. The rare parts of human writing get thinner, the new model’s writing gets narrower, and a following model trained on that output is narrower still.

PART B

Scale: compute, data, and parameters

Three knobs each improve next-token prediction when you turn them up: more compute, more training text, and more parameters. From about 2019 through 2024, models kept getting larger for that reason. A bigger model is more sample-efficient: the same number of tokens reaches a lower loss in a larger model. The open question was how to spend a fixed compute budget between size and data.

FLOPs (lowercase s) is a count of floating-point operations: how much work the run does. FLOPS (all caps, or written “per second”) is how fast the hardware can do that work. The two get swapped in papers. The sentence decides which one is meant. A training run of order 1025 FLOPs is a statement about work, not about the speed of one chip.

Work versus speed

There is no single formula. The total depends on the architecture as well as on the token count and the parameter count, and it sits on the order of the product of those two. A large run was already put on the order of 1025 FLOPs. Frontier runs on 31 October 2026 are at that scale and above. This page does not pin a 2026 total.

A mixture of experts spends less compute per token than a dense model with the same total parameter count, because only some experts run. The scaling discussion in this session is about decoder-only transformers. Within that family, tokens and size dominate small architecture tweaks.

The product shape is why “twice the data” and “twice the parameters” are both expensive, and why a fixed budget must choose a split. You cannot turn all three knobs without bound.

The 2020 scaling fits (Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models) varied compute, data, and size, and found smooth improvement in next-token loss. Their allocation of a fixed budget favored growing the model. The Chinchilla fit redid that allocation. For a fixed training compute budget, a strong split is about 20 tokens of training text per parameter.

PART C

One update, many devices

The model is initialized at billions to hundreds of billions of parameters for a dense run. A mixture of experts can list more than it activates. One device cannot hold the training state of a frontier model. The concrete device in the worked example is an H100 with 80 gigabytes. Newer devices hold more, still far below a full frontier training state, so the split remains.

The forward pass computes the loss and produces activations, the numbers at each layer that the backward pass will need. The backward pass computes the gradient, the direction that reduces the loss, one number per parameter. The optimizer turns that direction into a change in the parameters, and it may store extra numbers of its own to do it.

What one step has to store

Activation memory depends on the model size, the batch size, and the context length. Self-attention grows with the square of the sequence length. Contexts far past the 2020 plots are why activation memory, not only the parameters, forces a split across devices and a careful attention kernel.

Activations

Saved on the forward pass so the backward pass can apply the chain rule. They grow with batch and with context. FlashAttention, in the next part, is largely about not keeping all of them.

Gradients

One direction per parameter. Data parallelism averages these directions across devices before the update.

Optimizer state

Adam keeps two running averages per parameter. That state is often the largest extra store, which is why sharding it is the first win.

The optimizer: AdamW is usual, Muon is a candidate

Adam keeps a first moment, a moving average of the gradient, and a second moment, a moving average of the squared gradient. The step follows the first and is shrunk by the square root of the second, so a steady direction takes a moderate step and a rare spike is damped. Both averages are stored.

The form people ship on 31 October 2026 is AdamW: the same two moments, with weight decay applied on the weights themselves rather than folded into the gradient. Muon takes a momentum update and orthogonalizes it. It is a real candidate, used in some runs, and it is not the established default. Adafactor stores less optimizer state, to save memory. SGD keeps no moments. Both are side paths. They have not replaced AdamW.

A raw gradient is a noisy direction from one batch. The two averages keep a memory of recent directions so the update is less at the mercy of a single batch. The memory is the cost: two more numbers per parameter, usually in high precision.

Data parallelism and ZeRO

Data parallelism keeps a full copy of the model on each device and gives each device its own slice of the batch. The memory tied to the batch shrinks. The gradient that is applied is the average of the per-device gradients. That average is communication between devices, and it slows the run. One full model still has to fit on a device, until the copies themselves are partitioned.

ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer) exists because those copies duplicate parameters, gradients, and optimizer state. It is still data parallelism: each device has its own data. The three stages partition more of the duplicated state.

PART D

FlashAttention: exact attention, fewer memory trips

The 2022 method speeds up attention without approximating the math. The result matches ordinary attention. The change is where the intermediate numbers live, and whether the backward pass stores them.

HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is the large memory quoted for the device: tens of gigabytes, relatively slow, on the order of a few terabytes per second in this account. SRAM sits on the chip next to the compute: several tens of megabytes, and about ten times faster, at tens of terabytes per second. The arithmetic of attention is not the bottleneck. The reads and writes of the intermediates were.

What ordinary attention writes out

softmax( Q Kᵀ / √d ) V

Ordinary attention writes the query-key product, then the softmax, then the multiply by V, back to HBM. Softmax is row-wise: each row sums to 1, which looks as if the whole row must exist before any output is valid. That appearance is what tiling has to respect without materializing the row.

Tiling that stays exact

Tiling sends small blocks to SRAM, finishes the block there, and writes the output back once. The identity that makes this exact: the softmax of a row split into blocks equals the softmax of those blocks up to a scaling factor, because the normalizer is shared along the row. Each query slice is paired with the matching key and value slices. The scaling quantities are exact and are updated as the output block is filled. They are not an approximation, and the factor’s algebraic form is not the part to memorize. A tile may be a portion of a row. Think of the attention matrix as a grid. The input-output pattern aimed at is a single read of the inputs from HBM, the block work in SRAM, and a write of the result.

SRAM is too small for a long row, and HBM is too slow to use as a scratchpad. The block math lets a tiny fast memory produce the same row softmax the full matrix would have produced.

A worked comparison gave HBM traffic of 40.3 for the ordinary method, and almost a tenfold cut for the tiled method, with a shorter runtime as well. The second raw figure was left unfinished, so this page does not invent it. FlashAttention 2 and 3 retarget the same idea to later GPUs. On 31 October 2026 the usual kernel for exact attention is this family: tiled, exact, aware of the two memories, including vendor kernels that implement the same pattern.

Store the intermediates, or compute them again

The backward pass needs activations. They are cheap to rebuild relative to storing every attention intermediate, so they can be dropped after the forward loss and computed again on the way back. That does more arithmetic. Recomputing usually spends time to save memory. Here the memory traffic was the bottleneck, so both the traffic and the runtime dropped.

PART E

Mixed precision and quantization during the run

Quantization changes how many bits represent a number, to save memory and to go faster, while hoping the loss still falls the same way. Mixed precision is the training recipe that uses a cheap format for the arithmetic and a richer format for the stored weights.

A floating-point number spends its bits on three jobs. The sign is one bit. The exponent sets the range, how large or small the number can be. The mantissa sets the granularity, how finely two nearby numbers can be told apart. Fewer mantissa bits means a coarser grid. Fewer exponent bits means a number overflows or underflows sooner.

The mixed-precision step

The original mixed-precision recipe keeps the weights in FP32, runs the forward and backward operations in FP16, and applies the weight update in FP32. The reported result was little loss of quality, less memory, and a faster run. It is not required on every weight of every layer. Groups pick the mix the way they pick the token ratio: a small run, then an extrapolation.

A batch is already noisy, so the update mainly needs a direction. The weights stay in high precision so rounding error does not pile up across many steps. The fast format is for the arithmetic that is redone every token.

On 31 October 2026 the usual recipe is the same shape with BF16 in the fast path: compute the forward and backward passes in BF16, keep a higher-precision master copy of the weights, and apply the update to that copy. FP16 mixed precision still works, especially on older chips, and it needs the loss scaling that BF16 usually avoids. Pure FP32 training is the original form. It remains correct, and it is the slow, memory-heavy side path for a full-scale run.

Quantizing the forward pass

Train-time quantization pushes that same idea further: store or compute some of the forward pass in fewer bits, and keep the update more precise so the error does not accumulate. FP8 compute is the version of this that is actually used on recent hardware for some large runs. It sits beside BF16. It is not the only usual choice.

Zero-point and absmax are ways to place the numeric range when values are squeezed into a coarse grid. They are range tricks. They are not ZeRO, and they are not the main training recipe. Four-bit storage of a frozen base, including NF4, belongs to fine-tuning in the next session, where the base is not being updated. Pretraining still updates every weight, which is why the master copy stays in a richer format.

PART A

Supervised fine-tuning and instruction tuning

Supervised fine-tuning starts from the pretrained weights and trains on pairs of inputs and outputs. The next-token objective stays. It is applied to the answer, and the prompt is held fixed.

Fine-tuning means more training steps on weights that already exist. The model is not initialized from scratch. Instruction tuning is the supervised mix that turns a continuer into an assistant: the input is a request, and the output is a response. It is a choice of data, not a new loss.

The loss starts at the answer

loss = − log P(next answer token | prompt + answer so far)

Pretraining starts at the beginning-of-sequence token and predicts through the whole document, with the true prefix shown at every step. In supervised fine-tuning the prompt is a condition. The tokens of the prompt are not targets. Worked case: a dirty teddy bear, and whether it can go in the washer. The pretrained model continues into what the bear is made of. After this stage the target is an answer, and the helpful one in the worked case is to hand-wash the bear rather than machine-wash it. The loss starts at the first token of that answer.

The prompt is the user’s words. Training the model to copy the prompt wastes the step on text it will be given anyway. The behavior you want to install lives in the answer.

The mix that makes an assistant

The training mix is prompts plus targets. The categories are story writing, poem writing, lists, explanations, math and proofs, high-quality code, and a safety slice, among others. The aim stated for a general assistant is helpful and harmless. The safety slice is there so the model does not repeat harmful text from pretraining. Refusals live in the weights. A refusal written as a regular expression is rare, because a pattern list does not cover the ways a harmful request can be phrased. Hedging is how the targets avoid a blanket claim when the prompt does not deserve one.

The supervised set is unbalanced, low quality, or missing the kind of prompt users actually send. Preference tuning does not repair that. If the tuned model misbehaves often, inspect this set first.
  • Human time is the cost. A finished set is reused and extended. Later sets mix human text with model generations that a checker kept.
  • A prompt close to the training prompts can generalize. A poetry prompt near the stories in the set is the close case. A story drawn from movie plots, far from those stories, may not. The remedy is more data spread through that region, rather than one story repeated.

PART B

Preference data and the reward model

Writing one great poem is harder than pointing at the better of two poems. Supervised fine-tuning only shows what to generate. A pair can say that the other answer is worse. Adding one corrective paragraph to the supervised set can tilt a carefully balanced mix, and that set is supposed to stay very high quality, so stuffing every mistake into it is slow.

A preference pair is one prompt, two answers, and a judgment of which answer is better. A reward is a number for a finished answer. A policy is the model’s distribution over the next token. In this loop the language model is the agent, the tokens so far are the state, and the next token is the action.

Prompts come from logs or from a set you wish the model handled. Two samples at a positive temperature give two different answers from the same prompt. The judge can be a person or a model. The two names are the same two steps with a different judge. Supervised fine-tuning cannot forbid a sentence unless that sentence is rewritten into a good target. The pair is the negative signal.

The loop, in reinforcement-learning words

The reward arrives for the finished answer, about one signal per completion, against a token-by-token target in supervised fine-tuning. The signal is called sparse for that reason. The parameters to learn are the policy’s, so that the next-token distribution lines up with the preferences. The model being updated is the one that already went through pretraining and supervised fine-tuning.

A token-level target needs someone to write the right token. A preference only needs a finished answer and a comparison. The price is a weaker, later signal: the model is not told which token spoiled the answer.

What the reward model is

Step one learns a score for a prompt plus a response. The set is tens of thousands of pairs or more, with human labels when the name is human feedback. Step two freezes that score and uses it to change the policy. A decoder-only model with a head on the last state is the usual reward model. The head is often called a classification head, and the number it emits is still a continuous score. An encoder that scores a classification token is the older form. RewardBench is a widely used check of these models. This page does not quote a score on it.

A score is for one dimension: useful, friendly, safe, or one combined number. Rater guidelines have to be as objective as the task allows, or the labels are noisy. The training target is better-or-worse, so the setup looks like classification, and the number that comes out is continuous and is rescaled on the batch. It is not a fixed scale such as one to five. A spoken illustration is about 1 for a good answer and about −2 or −3 for a bad one. Treat those magnitudes as a sketch of “up” and “down.”

Reward hacking, and the tether to the supervised model

Step two feeds a prompt, lets the model write a full completion (a rollout), and takes a number back from the frozen reward model, for example −2, not a thumbs-down. That number updates the language model. The goal is a higher reward without leaving the starting model. Three reasons hold the step back. The starting model already knows a lot, and a large step forgets it. The reward model is wrong in places. Training can also go unstable.

The score is how loud the applause is. Joke-telling wins, and the talk gets less informative. Chasing a flawed score past the point where it still matches the real goal is reward hacking.

This stage uses at least 100,000 completions, labeled by the reward model, starting from the supervised model. The loss has a reward piece and a piece that keeps the policy near a reference. The reference here is the supervised model.

KL is a gap between distributions

KL(p ‖ q) = Σ p_i log(p_i / q_i)

The sum is at least zero by Jensen’s inequality, and it is zero only when the two distributions match. Swapping p and q changes the value, so the number is not used as a distance. In this stack, p is the policy being trained and q is the reference, and the term is a penalty for drifting away from the supervised model. A coefficient, often called β, sets how hard that penalty pulls.

PART C

From a clipped update to a direct loss, then to a checker

The reward model is optional machinery. The thing you wanted was a policy that prefers the better answer and stays near the supervised model. Three algorithms chase that, and they keep different numbers of extra models in memory.

Advantage: the reward, compared with what you expected

A raw reward has high variance. Subtracting a baseline, the reward you expected for this situation, leaves the surprise, which is the advantage. The value function estimates, at a partial generation, the reward you would get if you finished under the current policy. It is token-level, trained jointly, and it is a regression head. The formula used in practice is generalized advantage estimation. The algebra is not derived here. The point to hold is that the baseline cuts variance and speeds training.

The clip answers a different fear from the KL. The clip stops one iteration from moving the token probabilities too far. The KL stops the policy from wandering away from the supervised model that already knew the language.

Reasoning: a checker, then a group-relative update

Open-ended preference has no automatic right answer, so it keeps a judge or a pair. Math and code often do: the number is correct, or the tests pass. That reward is verifiable. The usual algorithm for this stage on 31 October 2026 is group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). Several completions are drawn for one prompt. Each one’s advantage is its reward compared with the others in the group, so a learned value head is unnecessary, and a learned reward model is unnecessary when the checker exists. Proximal policy optimization was the earlier default for the policy update. GRPO has not replaced direct preference, or a reward model, on prompts where nothing can be checked.

A checker is a reward you do not have to learn, so the failure mode of applause-for-jokes is smaller. The group supplies the baseline that a value head used to supply. The stage still comes after supervised fine-tuning: the model has to be able to attempt the problem before a checker can prefer the attempts that succeed.

The usual order on 31 October 2026 is the flow at the top of the session. Pretrain, then supervised fine-tuning, then preference by reinforcement learning from feedback or by a direct method, then this reasoning stage. GRPO is the reasoning algorithm in that last box. It is not a replacement for teaching the model to follow an instruction.

PART D

LoRA and QLoRA

Supervised fine-tuning and preference do not have to touch every weight. The usual way to adapt a model, when you are not updating the whole base, is a low-rank change added beside the frozen weights.

Rank is the inner width of that change. A matrix of rank R can be written as a product of a tall thin matrix and a short wide one. LoRA trains only those two factors. The original weight matrix stays frozen. QLoRA stores the frozen matrix in a 4-bit format and trains the factors in a richer format.

Two empirical notes, with the offered guesses left as guesses. Use a learning rate about ten times higher than a full fine-tune: a small rank has a smaller space to search, and a larger step covers it. Larger batches work less well: a product of two matrices does not train like a full matrix. Those guesses were not derived. The observations are the part to keep.

Most of a pretrained matrix is already useful. The task needs a small correction. Training the correction, and leaving the rest frozen, is why many tasks can share one base.

NF4, and the second quantization

NF4 (normal-float 4-bit) assumes the frozen weights are roughly normal and splits the range into quantiles rather than equal-width buckets, so each bin holds about the same number of values. The bits are spent where the weights actually sit. The factors A and B stay in BF16 and receive the gradients. Double quantization then quantizes the constants that convert between the full weights and the 4-bit weights. A memory saving is the point. A further, smaller saving comes from the second quantization. This page does not treat any single multiplier as a clean figure for that saving.

Zero-point and absmax, from the training session, are other ways to place a coarse grid. NF4’s quantile grid is the usual one for this frozen base. The base is frozen, which is why a 4-bit copy is tolerable: the rounding error is not being stepped for thousands of updates of W₀ itself.

Full fine-tunes, and the methods that stayed smaller

A full fine-tune updates every weight. It still happens when the owner has the weights and the budget. LoRA has not erased it. LoRA is the usual way to adapt a model without touching every weight, including when the preference loss and the supervised loss are the objectives being optimized. The two levers combine: a low-rank change can be trained with either loss.

Prefix tuning and adapters are other ways to add a small trained piece while freezing the base. They are less used. They have not replaced LoRA. On 31 October 2026 the default adaptation is LoRA, or QLoRA when memory is tight enough that the base has to live in 4 bits. The full update remains the side path you take when you own the model and you can pay to move every parameter.

pass@k and pass@1

pass@k is the chance that at least one of k tries is right. It matters when you can afford extra tries and you can check them, as in code. It is the checkable cousin of best-of-n, which needed a learned reward model to pick a winner. Estimating pass@k from only k draws is noisy. Draw n answers, of which c are correct. The chance that k draws without replacement are all wrong is the binomial ratio below. pass@k is 1 minus that chance.

pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)   ·   pass@1 = c / n

When k is 1, C(n − c, 1) / C(n, 1) = (n − c) / n, so pass@1 is the fraction of successful attempts. The draws are without replacement, and k cannot exceed n. If fewer than k answers are wrong, every set of k contains a success and the estimate is 1.

PART D

Train on the checker, and compare a group

Do not start from handwritten chains. Long chains are hard to write, and the model may not reason the way a person does, so a human trace can be the wrong teacher. Math and code already have a reward: tests pass, or the answer matches.

PPO (proximal policy optimization, 2017) was the default. It trains the policy and a second model, the value model, that guesses how good the future looks. GRPO (group-relative policy optimization, 2024) skips that second model. It samples several answers to the same prompt, scores them with the checker, and treats “better than the others in this group” as the advantage.
An easy problem can look high-reward because it is easy. A correct answer on a hard problem should be up-weighted more. Comparing inside the group puts that into the advantage. The same comparison removes the need to train a value function.

GRPO compared with PPO

Both still maximize advantage. Both stay near the previous iteration by clipping the probability ratio, and both stay near a frozen reference with a KL term. In this reasoning setup neither one uses a learned reward model. The check is a test or a ground-truth answer.

Ai = ( ri − mean(r) ) / std(r)

Length bias, and the factor later losses drop

During this training, average length rises with the reasoning chain, and performance rises with it, until performance flattens and length keeps growing. Inside the original objective, each token is scaled by 1 over the length of its own completion.

weight of a token in completion i = 1 / |oi|

A token in a short answer weighs more than the same token in a long answer. When the advantage is negative, a short bad answer is punished harder than a long bad answer, so long bad answers are preferred. That is the account of the runaway length.

Wrong answers get longer because a long wrong answer is punished less, per token, than a short wrong answer. Performance has already flattened. You pay for tokens that do not raise the checker’s score.

Later losses drop that per-length factor. Two forms showed up in early 2025 and are part of what people ship:

  • DAPO (March 2025) uses one shared normalizer, so every token weighs the same.
  • Dr. GRPO drops the factor. Correct answers stay about as long as before. Incorrect answers get much shorter. That is the bottom-right chart of that comparison. No cell value is read out.

A third change uses different clip widths. The ratio is held near (1 + ε) times the old probability. A rare token cannot grow if ε is small, because a small cap multiplies a tiny probability. A large ε lets a likely token fall to zero. The bounds are made asymmetric so a rare token can rise without letting a common token collapse. The best widths are not settled here. STILL OPEN

Usual on 31 October 2026: group-relative updates, no value model, and a loss that does not divide each token by its own completion length. PPO remains the earlier default for learned preferences. Asymmetric clips and a softened spread are common companions. They have not been reduced to one canonical formula.

Check yourself

1 · Why is pass@k estimated from n draws with c successes, and what does the formula become when k is 1?
k draws alone are a noisy estimate of a rate. Draw a larger pool of n answers, count c successes, and use sampling without replacement. pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k). When k is 1 that ratio is (n − c) / n, so pass@1 = c / n, the fraction of successful attempts.
2 · How does group-relative advantage avoid a value model, and why does dividing each token’s contribution by its own completion length favor long bad answers?
The baseline is the other samples for the same prompt. Advantage is (reward − group mean) / group spread, so nothing has to predict the return. Each token is then weighted by 1 over its own completion length, so a token in a short answer weighs more. A negative advantage therefore punishes a short wrong answer harder than a long wrong answer, and long wrong answers are preferred. Later losses drop that per-length factor. Correct answers stay about as long. Incorrect answers get much shorter.
3 · What does R1-Zero skip, what goes wrong in its chains, and how is the smaller-model copy different from matching a teacher’s full next-token distribution?
R1-Zero skips supervised fine-tuning and runs the checker loop on a base model. The chains mix languages and break syntax, because nothing anchored the format. The smaller-model copy is supervised fine-tuning on traces the teacher already wrote, including the think tokens. Older distillation matched the teacher’s full distribution over the next token, not one written sequence.

PART B

Retrieve, then augment, then generate

A question such as who won the local election is paired with the passage that actually says who won. That pair is what the model sees. RAG is retrieve, augment, generate.

Split the library into chunks. Turn each chunk into a vector. Turn the question into a vector. Keep the chunks whose vectors point a similar way. Then let a heavier scorer reorder that short list. The generator only reads the winners.
The generator is strong at writing from the text in front of it and weak at knowing which page of a library matters. Retrieval’s job is to put the right page in front of it. If the page is wrong, a fluent answer is still wrong.

Build a store of chunks, then embed them

  • Collect the documents that might help. Split each into chunks of a maximum length, typically on the order of hundreds of tokens. About 500 is a working size. Too short and the piece has no context. Too long and one vector cannot represent it.
  • Overlap neighbors by a span in the low hundreds of tokens, so a sentence that starts in one chunk can finish in the next.
  • A naive split ignores structure. JSON and Markdown need their own rules. Those rules are separate from the retrieval math and are not derived here.
  • Embedding width is typically in the thousands. About 1,500 is an example width. Wider vectors hold more nuance and cost more space and more work at query time.
  • People usually start from a pretrained embedding model. Training one is the other option. The contrastive loss that trains it is the subject of Sentence-BERT, and it is not derived here.

A chunk taken out of its document can be nonsense. One fix writes a short context from the whole document and prepends it, one model call per chunk. Prompt caching, in the next part, is what makes that affordable: the document prefix is repeated, so its activations can be reused.

Candidate retrieval is a similarity search

Two stages. Recall shrinks a huge set to something like a hundred or more possibly relevant chunks, and it optimizes recall: miss as few true hits as you can afford. Ranking, sometimes skipped, reorders that short list with a heavier model. Embeddings mainly affect the first stage.

The usual recall architecture is a bi-encoder. An encoder-only model, often BERT-like, runs on the query and on the chunk separately. Sentence-BERT is the paper that turns BERT into one vector per sequence, trained so relevant pairs have high cosine similarity and irrelevant pairs have low. Encode the query and each chunk, compare them, and keep the top of the list, on the order of the top 100. A huge base is not scanned linearly. Approximate nearest-neighbor indexes partition the vectors when the base is built. An exact scan is the side path for a small base.

cos(q, d) = (q · d) / (|q| |d|)

Cosine similarity is the usual comparison. It reads the angle, not the length. L2 distance shows up in code. If every vector has norm 1, ordering by L2 and ordering by cosine agree, and the dot product is enough. The original comparison people reach for is still the angle.

Semantic match and keyword match answer different queries

Two texts can mean the same thing and share no words. A pure embedding search is built for that. BM25 is a heuristic on word overlap, for queries where a keyword must appear. Two bears, Cuddly and Huggy, are semantically close, so a pure embedding search can return Huggy when the query is “where is Cuddly.”

PART C

Re-rank the short list, score it, and remember the prefix

Recall is allowed to be a bit sloppy if it keeps the true hits. The ranker spends a heavier model on about a hundred candidates, not on millions. The scores below are how you tell whether that order is any good.

A cross-encoder sees the query and the chunk together

The original second stage, and the one still used when quality is worth the cost, is a cross-encoder. It takes the query and the chunk in one pass and emits one relevance score. Attention runs between them. The bi-encoder never did that. Score every survivor, sort descending, and keep the top k that will enter the prompt. The ranker can be pretrained or trained. It can be heavier because the list is short.

Skipping the ranker is the cheaper side path. The bi-encoder’s order is then the order that enters the prompt. On 31 October 2026 the usual pipeline on a large store is still recall, then re-rank, with hybrid search in the recall stage when names must match. Long context did not replace this. It only made it reasonable to paste the top k in full.

A single vector is a summary. Two texts that interact token by token can disagree with that summary. The cross-encoder pays for the interaction only after recall has thrown most of the library away.

Four scores for a retriever

Labels are relevant or not, as in a binary classifier, and position matters. You care about the first k ranks because those chunks are the ones that enter the prompt. These four scores still mean what they meant when they were introduced. Nothing has replaced them. The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) is the named place to compute them and compare retrievers. This page does not rank those retrievers.

NDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k   ·   DCG@k = Σi=1..k reli / log2(i + 1)

With binary labels, reli is 1 or 0. The sum is the discounted cumulative gain: cumulative because it counts hits in the top k, discounted because rank 1 is worth more than rank k. The usual discount in libraries is the logarithm above. Divide by the ideal DCG for that query, the score of a perfect ranking, so a perfect ranking scores 1. The idea does not depend on memorizing the log. The log is the form the libraries use.

A decoder-only model is left to right, so a shared prefix produces the same activations every time. The original habit recomputes that prefix on every call. That is why a context line in front of every chunk can be cheap: the document is the prefix, and only the short new instruction changes. The same trick applies to a tool schema that sits at the front of every turn. Caching is a side path you skip when the prefix changes every call. It has not replaced retrieval. It makes a repeated preamble affordable.

Memory is what the next step can read

The weights do not update in the middle of a task. The original memory is the conversation pasted back into the next call. That paste is still the short memory: the user’s ask, the plan, and the tool results. It is why the model can turn a JSON blob into a sentence about the bear the user actually wanted.

Long memory is outside the weights. The chunk store is one form. A named resource, such as a shelf or a database a tool can open, is another. Usual on 31 October 2026: keep the recent transcript verbatim, and search the store or the resource for anything that does not fit. When the transcript outgrows the window, some runners replace older turns with a summary. That summary is a side path. It has not replaced the recent turns or the store.

The cache is treated as memory of the user’s facts. It only remembers activations of a prefix. A new fact still has to be retrieved or returned by a tool. A summary of old turns can also drop the one constraint the later steps needed.
PART D

The model names the call. Something outside runs it.

A tool is a function the model can ask to run. Structured data, a table of inputs and outputs, is the same idea in function form. The examples are written in Python because that is easy to read. Nothing requires Python. The functions are written ahead of time. They are not invented during the call.

The model sees the name, the argument types, and a short description. It does not see the code. It fills in the arguments. A program outside the model runs the function. The structured result comes back, and the model writes a sentence.

One quoted scope is enough: tool calling lets a system finish a task by reaching data and taking actions, including resources outside the model. The outside part is optional. A local calculation is still a tool.

Three steps, and the bear near you

Without a tool, “find a teddy bear near me” ends in “I do not know.” With permission to read a location, the model can pass coordinates. The function returns a structured object: a name, a place, and so on.

Pair one: the API plus the query, mapping to the arguments. Pair two: the whole history, including the tool result, mapping to the final sentence. A strong code model may not need that fine-tuning. Two substitutes exist. Few-shot examples in the window are one, and they can fail to generalize. The other is a fixed explanation of how to call the function, written offline. Use the supervised pairs as an evaluation set, score the current explanation, and ask a reasoning model to rewrite the explanation from the wins and the losses. Do not write that explanation end to end by hand. At inference the explanation is frozen next to the API. That rewrite is training-time prompt work, not a loop on every query.

Usual now: the schema is the contract, and a short description sits beside it. Fine-tuning on the two pairs remains the side path when a smaller model does not follow the schema. The hand-written essay of instructions is the form that lost to “score it, then let a reasoning model rewrite it.”

Lookup, calculation, and action

Lookup

News of the day, weather, and stocks come from a search or a feed, not from the weights. The tool returns text or a record. The model writes from that record.

Calculation

A calculation can be turned into code, executed, and read back. That sits beside a reasoning chain from session 06. The code path is usual when a runtime can check the result. The chain remains usual when the hard part is the plan.

Action

Sending mail means filling the header and the body and hitting send. The tool touches the outside world. A wrong argument is no longer only a wrong sentence.

Too many tools, and a selector

A real preamble holds many functions: find a bear, hug one, check a mood, send a gift. Too many, and a needle-in-a-haystack failure hides the right API. Conflicting APIs compete. A finite window cannot hold a tool for every user of a service with hundreds of millions of people. Each vendor’s format was also its own dialect.

A selector uses two steps. The first prompt lists only names and a word or two, and the model picks. The second prompt receives only those full schemas. Routing is the same idea. Retrieval over tool descriptions is a possible way to build the selector. It is not required. STILL OPEN whether the selector itself should be a retriever. The usual pattern that did settle is: do not put every schema in the window; narrow first, then show the chosen ones.

One description of a tool, shared

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is the named standard so the same tool is not rewritten per model. An MCP server serves three kinds of thing. Tools are the functions. Prompts are templates that show how to use them. Resources are outside databases, which is long memory with a name. Each server pairs with one MCP client on the host.

Worked shape, not a product ranking: the host is a chat app, the server is a book provider, the tools find or recommend books, the prompts cover a title or a taste, and the resources are a personal shelf or a bestseller list. Custom schemas remain a side path inside one product. MCP is the usual shared description when the tool and the model are built apart.

PART E

An agent is a tool user that loops until a goal is met

An agent is a system that pursues a goal and finishes tasks on a user’s behalf. The extra piece, compared with one tool call, is reasoning and more than one iteration. The loops can contain the chains from session 06. They are not a separate world.

One tool call is ask, run, answer. A loop is: look at what you know, decide the next action, run it, read what came back, and either stop or go around again. Coding agents and research agents work this way. Each extra lap is another chance to misread a result.

PART A

Human raters, and why raw agreement is luck

A person on every answer is the grade you want. Cost and time rule it out, and two careful people still disagree on a fuzzy question.

A rater is a person who marks an answer good or bad. Agreement is the share of items where two raters give the same mark. Some of those matches happen even if each person is flipping a coin with a fixed habit.

The worked fuzziness is a gift question. “What birthday gift should I get?” answered with “a teddy bear is almost always a sweet gift” is useful to a reader who wanted a safe idea, and useless to a reader who wanted one specific animal. Both marks can be sincere. A thousand outputs makes a person on every item too slow. Tracking agreement does not remove that cost. It tells you whether the rubric is stable enough to trust the labels you did buy.

Chance agreement

The raw agreement rate is the fraction of items where the two marks match. It is a bad number by itself. If rater A says “good” with probability pA and rater B with probability pB, and they choose independently, they match in two ways: both say good, or both say bad.

pe = pA pB + (1 − pA)(1 − pB)

pe is the agreement you expect from those habits alone. At equal 0.5 rates, pe = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5. Half the matches are luck. A higher base rate of “good” raises that floor: people who almost always say good will match often, even when they are not reading the same way.

Cohen’s kappa

Cohen’s kappa puts the observed agreement po next to that floor. It is the original correction people still use for two raters. The algebraic shape is the excess agreement, scaled so that perfect agreement is 1.

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)
  • κ is positive when the raters beat chance.
  • κ is 1 when they always agree (po = 1).
  • κ is 0 when po = pe. The worked 0.5 / 0.5 case with a 50% match rate has κ = 0.
  • κ is negative when they agree less than chance.

More than two raters use Fleiss’s kappa or Krippendorff’s alpha. Those are the same idea with a different count of who could have matched. They sit beside Cohen. They did not replace it for a pair of raters.

Teams track the number and hold an alignment session when it is low, so the rubric means one thing. That repairs subjectivity. A person on every new answer is still too expensive, which is why the next grades exist. Kappa itself has not been replaced: it still means agreement above chance.
PART B

A frozen reference, and why n-gram overlap fails

The next attempt writes the ideal answer once and scores later models against that frozen text. The metric has to allow more than one good wording. Overlap of short word-chunks is the original way to do that. It still fails when the right answer is a paraphrase.

An n-gram is a run of n words. A unigram is one word. A bigram is two words in a row. Precision asks what share of the model’s pieces appear in the reference. Recall asks what share of the reference’s pieces appear in the model’s text. A reference is the frozen answer you compare against.

METEOR, the one that cares about order

METEOR is Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit Ordering. It was built for translation. It is an F-score, so both precision and recall are in it, times one minus a penalty for broken order. Higher is better. Synonyms and shared roots count as matches, which is wider than exact words, and still far short of a paraphrase.

P = matched / predicted unigrams    R = matched / reference unigrams
F = P R / (α P + (1 − α) R)    penalty = γ (chunks / matches)β
METEOR = F × (1 − penalty)

α, β, and γ are free constants. They are chosen, not derived. Precision is the share of predicted unigrams that match the reference. Recall is the share of reference unigrams that match the prediction. A chunk is a contiguous run of those matches. Few chunks means the matches are long, the order agrees, and the penalty should be small. Many chunks means the same words showed up in a scrambled order.

pass@k and passk

You run the task n times and count c successes. Then you look at a sample of k of those tries, drawn without replacement. pass@k is the chance that at least one of the k succeeded. passk is the chance that all k succeeded. The second is the reliability number for an agent, because a booking that works once in five tries is not a working booking.

passk = C(c, k) / C(n, k)   when k ≤ c, else 0
pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)

C(n, k) is the number of ways to choose k tries out of n. The algebra is that count. It is the sampling-without-replacement argument, not a fitted score.

Contamination and Goodhart

A benchmark only measures what the model has not already seen. Once the set is a target, training and even the wording of prompts bend toward it, and the number stops meaning “can it do the job.”

  • The original defense is secrecy and a fresh exam. New math exams are the clean version of that, because the answers did not exist at training time.
  • Hashes of the test items, and a blocklist of sites that contain the answers for tool tasks, are the defenses used when the set is already public.
  • A public arena of human votes is a check against a contaminated leaderboard. People are comparing the answers in front of them, not a frozen set that may have been in the training text.
  • The last check is to run the model on the actual task.
The judge has the same trap. It is a proxy for people. Tuning until the judge score rises, while agreement with people falls, is Goodhart applied to the grader. Human labels, when you can afford a sample, are how you notice the drift. The arena is how a public ranking gets a vote that is not the benchmark.

A model is a profile

Plot quality against price, or against safety, or against context length. The outer edge of the models that are best on that tradeoff is the Pareto frontier. One model can be the right pick when code quality dominates. Another can be the right pick when speed and price dominate. Those are different axes. A single rank erases the axis.

As of 31 October 2026 the shipped question is which profile you need, not which name sits at the top of one table. This session names no winner and quotes no leaderboard score.

PART B

About 20 tokens per parameter, and a faster exact attention

Early plots show test loss falling as compute, data, and parameter count grow. Lower is better. Compute is finite, so the live question is how to split a budget, and how to run the same attention without drowning in slow memory.

Parameters are the numbers the model stores. Tokens are the pieces of text it reads in training. A GPU has a large slow memory and a small fast one. Attention wants a huge table of scores. The fast memory cannot hold that table for a long sequence.

The training-compute split

Plots from the early 2020s, with test loss on the vertical axis, improve as compute, data, and parameter count grow. Given a compute budget, most models of that time were too large for their data: too many parameters for the tokens they saw. The rule of thumb that came out is the reference floor people still use.

tokens ≥ 20 × parameters
100 billion × 20 = 2 trillion

A 100-billion-parameter model wants at least 2 trillion tokens, because 100 billion times 20 is 2 trillion. Train well below that ratio and, on this account, the model is undertrained.

About 20 tokens per parameter is still the floor for the question “did this model see enough text for its size?” A second question is now just as common: “what is cheap to serve at a target quality?” The usual answer there is a smaller model trained past that floor, because the extra tokens are paid once and every later reply is cheaper. This session does not name a new ratio. The floor was not repealed. Exceeding it is a serving decision that sits beside the original optimum.

Exact attention that touches slow memory less often

The math is the same scaled dot product as the block. The cost that dominates is traffic. A GPU’s large memory (high-bandwidth memory) is slow next to a small fast memory (SRAM). The tiled method runs each tile to completion in the fast memory, then writes back. It is exact. A second idea throws the intermediate scores away and recomputes them. More arithmetic, less traffic, and a faster run. The thing recomputed is that score table, so the full matrix of attention weights is not stored.

Data parallelism

Split the examples across processors. Each processor runs the same model on different rows of the batch. This is the usual first split when the model fits on one chip.

Model parallelism

Split one forward pass. The model itself is too wide or too deep for one chip, so different processors hold different pieces and pass activations.

The tiled exact kernel is the usual attention implementation. Approximate and sparse attention, including a sliding window, remain side paths for when the full look is too wide. They did not replace the exact product inside ordinary context lengths.

The grade on open text

BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR punish a wording that is different and still right. The usual grade is another model. It takes the prompt, the response, and the criteria, writes a rationale, then passes or fails. The rationale comes first for the same reason a reasoning model writes the chain first. The score is binary because that is easier to apply and less noisy than a fine scale. Position bias prefers whichever answer was shown first. Verbosity bias prefers the longer one. Self-enhancement bias prefers the judge’s own style. Public comparisons also report knowledge, reasoning, coding, and safety. That list is not complete. Session 08 is the full account, including a low temperature and a judge that is not the writer.

Patches, then a class

Self-attention does not require the vectors to be text. For a class label, keep the encoder, the piece that already worked for classification. A 2020 model, the vision transformer, splits the image into fixed patches and turns each patch into a vector.

A 3 by 3 patch, so the flatten is visible TOY

A pixel has three numbers: red, green, and blue. A 3 by 3 patch is 27 numbers. A learned linear map sends those 27 numbers to the width the encoder expects. The original vision transformer used patches on the order of 16 pixels on a side. That is the shipped magnitude. The 3 by 3 cut is only the shape.

The classification token

A classification token and a position vector are added, as in a text encoder. Every patch attends to every other patch. The classification token’s output has therefore seen the whole image. A feed-forward layer maps it to a class. The worked image is a teddy bear, and the desired class is teddy bear.

Convolutional networks slide a small window across the image, which matches how a person looks, and that inductive bias was the expected winner. The vision transformer has almost none of that bias. Trained on a lot of images, it beat those networks: enough data lets the model learn the bias instead of being given it. The patch is still how an image enters a transformer. A model whose only job is to name the image is no longer the main product.

Images, the usual generator

Image generators start from noise and refine the whole frame. A Gaussian is easy to draw, it supplies variety, and it is mathematically convenient. The forward process adds noise to a clean image until it is noise. The model learns to predict the noise to remove. The sculptor line is only that analogy: the sculpture is already complete in the marble, and the work is to chisel away the superfluous. The original denoiser was convolutional. Putting a transformer in that loop improved results, and a transformer is the usual block inside the refiner now. Flow matching is a widely shipped cousin of the same picture: start from noise and refine. Diffusion remains the form this session teaches.

Text, a side path

Tokens are discrete, so there is no Gaussian to add. The stand-in is the mask token. The forward process masks more and more positions until the sequence is all masks. The model learns to unmask them back into the sentence. At generation the prompt is given, the answer slots start masked, and the model fills them, including a word that sits after a word that has not been chosen yet. A useful picture is drafting a speech: a rough plan of the sections, then a refinement, coarse to fine.

The number of forward passes is the number of steps, and you choose it. It is typically much smaller than the number of tokens. More steps means higher quality. Some comparisons report a speedup around ten times, most useful on long outputs such as code, where the user is waiting and several calls stack. Treat that factor as a report from those comparisons, not a constant you can budget. Fill-in-the-middle fits this setup, because the model can use a left context and a right context together.

Autoregressive decoding is still the usual way a chat model writes. Masked diffusion is the side path that writes many tokens per step. Quality sat behind frontier autoregressive models for a while, and later papers have been closing that gap. Carrying a reasoning chain on this setup is open. Names you will see: masked diffusion model, and diffusion language model. The paper that walks through the math is LLaDA, Large Language Diffusion Models. The math is not derived here. Early public speed demonstrations included an experimental model from Google and a startup, Inception. Those demonstrations are about latency.

The two fields keep borrowing

Diffusion moved from images into text and, on that side path, cut the number of forward passes. In the other direction, image-diffusion papers replaced convolutions with the transformer and got better results. A paper that carries an optical-character-recognition name, DeepSeek-OCR, is mainly a map from a small number of vision tokens back to text tokens. The argument is that patches already carry the meaning, and a text tokenizer is a weak tool for that job: one emoji is one patch and would otherwise be many text tokens. Rotary position, which encodes relative distance along a line of text, is rewritten in two dimensions so a patch grid, or a mix of text tokens and image tokens, still has a relative position the attention score can use.

PART E

Model-written text, a cheaper frontier, and what is unsettled

The training text is often no longer written by people. The next edge, once public tests saturate, is cost at high quality. The block people ship is still not a proof that the block is the last design.

Curation, mid-training, and model collapse

Mid-training is a large corpus, cleaner than the raw scrape, sitting between pre-training and fine-tuning. Model collapse is what happens when a model is trained on text that models themselves wrote: the text is less diverse, the distribution shifts, and later learning gets worse.

The first models could scrape the web and expect human text, messy but human. A personal estimate now puts model-written pages at about 80 percent of the leading results of a search. ESTIMATE That figure is an estimate, not a measurement. Cite it only as an estimate.

The field’s response is curation, and the mid-training stage: still a lot of text, chosen for quality, before the supervised pairs. Training on the uncurated model-written stream is the failure called model collapse. The weights then imitate a narrower distribution than the human web they started from.

Small models as a cost frontier

If the benchmarks people care about saturate, the next edge of the tradeoff is cost at that quality. The small models in that literature are called small language models. Session 08’s profile is the same picture: quality against price, not a single rank. The architecture itself is not assumed to be the last one.

A simulated processor, not a chip you can buy

GPUs are built for a matrix product. The query-key product is expensive in memory traffic, which is why the tiled method recomputes instead of storing. No chip is named.

This is a side path in simulation. It is not a processor in a training run, and it does not change the block people ship.

Twenty times 100 billion is 2 trillion. Below that, the model has too many parameters for the tokens it saw, on the training-compute account. The tiled method recomputes the attention scores instead of storing the full score matrix in slow memory. The math stays the exact scaled dot product.
2 · How does a vision transformer turn a teddy-bear photo into a class, and why was beating a convolutional network surprising?
The photo is cut into patches, each patch is mapped to a vector, and a classification token attends to every patch, so its output has seen the whole image. A feed-forward layer maps that output to the class. Convolutional networks were handed a small sliding window, the bias people expected to win. The vision transformer was barely handed that bias, and enough data let it learn the bias anyway.
3 · Why can masked diffusion fill an answer in fewer forward passes than one pass per token, and what does a mask stand in for?
Each step can update many positions, and you choose how many steps to run, typically far fewer than the number of tokens. A mask stands in for Gaussian noise, because a token is discrete and you cannot add a Gaussian to it. Autoregressive chat is still the usual writer. This is the side path. A reported speedup around ten times is a comparison result, not a constant.
