PART A

Three ways to make test loss fall

Since GPT-3, a bigger base model has meant a lower test loss. Compute, data, and parameters are the three axes. The picture behind GPT-3, ChatGPT, PaLM, and Gemini is those three graphs.

Test loss is how surprised the model is by the next piece of text. Lower means the text was less of a surprise. It grades fit. It does not grade whether a person wanted that answer.

Compute, data, and parameters

Scaling laws are the name for this regular drop. Each axis, on its own, drives test loss down. More parameters here means more transformer layers. The training objective on the base model is still next-token prediction. That objective has not been replaced.

When the size chart stopped being the whole story

  • Original. From about 2018 to 2024, raising compute, data, and parameters was the plan. The size chart is that plan.
  • What replaced it, in order. The chart was called outdated once it was hitting saturation, around 2024. The next gains in this session come from a training stack (instructions and rankings), then from extra tries with the weights frozen, then from training on the tries that pass a check.
  • Usual on 31 Oct 2026. Frontier products still start with a large pretrained model. The behavior people use is the post-training plus a reasoning budget. Pretraining scale continues. It is no longer the only lever, and it is not a lever you turn by hand at answer time.
  • Side path. Pushing only the size chart, with no instruction stage and no answer-time budget, is how a base model is still trained. It is not how a chat or coding product is shipped.
PART B

Behaviors that showed up, and were not built as features

A larger model did more than post a lower loss. It started to follow a task from the prompt. These behaviors were noticed after the fact. They were not specified as features to install.

A prompt is the text you give the model before it writes. Zero-shot means the prompt names the task and then asks. Few-shot means the prompt also shows a few finished examples. Chain of thought means an example shows the steps, not only the final number.

Chain of thought: the steps sit in the example

The original trick is a human-written example. The example includes the arithmetic, not only the final number. The worked problem is small on purpose. A model with about 1 billion parameters solves it today with no example and no written steps. The trick mattered on hard problems, where a small model of that era could not use the steps.

Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 cans, and each can has 3. 5 + (2 × 3) = 5 + 6 = 11.
The example tells the model: start from 5, turn two cans into 6, add. A later question can reuse that shape. Writing “11” alone does not show the shape. A 1-billion-parameter model now answers this particular sum without being shown the shape. Harder problems are why the shape still matters.

PART C

Chat is a training stack on top of a large base model

The product that reached 1 million users in five days launched in November 2022. The jump past a raw GPT-3-style model was this stack. Size was already in the base model. The stack is what made it follow a person.

Pre-training practices “what comes next” on a huge mix of text. That model has statistics. It has no separate sense of an instruction. The later stages are where “answer the question, in the way a person prefers” gets into the weights.

PART D

Extra tries with the weights held fixed

Inference scaling leaves the weights fixed. The model is asked many times. A verifier keeps a correct sample. Large Language Monkeys is the repeated-sampling result: math and code, from 1 sample to 10,000.

The weights do not change while you sample. Temperature turns the variety of those samples up or down. A verifier is a separate yes-or-no. For code, the unit tests can be the verifier. Coverage is the fraction of problems where at least one sample is right.

PART E

Traces that pass a check go back into training

Self-improvement closes the loop. Test-time search writes traces that hit a known math answer or pass tests. Those traces are fine-tuning data. The model learns which chain is the good one, so the single committed answer gets better. Thinking models are this combination.

Sampling many times finds a needle and a verifier holds it up. The model itself has not changed, so the next single try is no wiser. Training on the traces that worked is the weight update. After that, one try is more often the right chain.

PART F

An agent is a goal with a loop

A chatbot answers inside a conversation. An agent is given a goal, plans steps, acts, reads feedback, and finishes or says it cannot. Tools sit outside the model. Memory keeps the task state.

Chat is a reply. An agent has a finish line. Search, a terminal, and a file edit are tools: the model asks, and something outside the weights does the action and returns a result. Memory is the agent’s note of what it has already done, so the next step can use it.

PART A

Where the compute is spent

Pre-training, fine-tuning, and inference. Only the third leaves the weights alone.

Pre-training fits the weights on a huge pile of text. Fine-tuning adjusts those weights on a much smaller pile. Inference is using the finished weights to answer. Test-time compute is extra work during inference: more tokens, more draws, or a check. The weights do not move.

Paid once, or paid on every question

Pre-training is the expensive stage: months, many GPUs, trillions of tokens, and a bill in the hundreds of millions or billions of dollars. Its curve predicts test loss from data, compute, and parameter count. The coefficients of that curve can change with the setup. Fine-tuning uses orders of magnitude less data, and much less money. A single inference call, when a call was one exchange, cost almost nothing beside those two.

The shift is that inference can now be a large bill on purpose. The extra tokens can run before anyone is waiting, because they do not need a weight update. The next question still starts from the same weights.

PART C

Coverage, and the chance one problem is hit

One problem has a short formula. A whole dataset has a different curve, and it needs a long tail of hard problems.

pass@1 is the chance a single draw is correct. Coverage is the fraction of problems solved by at least one sample. pass@k is the chance that, for one problem, at least one of k draws is correct.

One problem: pass@k

If one draw is correct with probability p, and the draws are independent, the chance the first k draws are all wrong is (1 − p) raised to k. One minus that is the chance at least one draw is right.

pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k

This formula has not been replaced. It is the per-problem curve. A dataset curve is a different object, below.

Across a dataset, coverage versus sample count k followed an exponential power law. Two coefficients, a and b, were fit to that curve. The dataset curve needs a long tail of hard problems with very low pass@1. A large share of problems are easy and already solved on the first draw. As problems get harder, pass@1 falls, and a thin set of problems stays very unlikely on one draw. That tail is the necessary shape. It was also the shape found when the problems were counted.

PART D

The generation–verification gap

The model can write a correct answer that the picker never selects.

A majority vote, also called self-consistency when each sample is a full solution, keeps the answer text that appears most often. Best-of-N ranks the finished samples with a reward model and keeps the top one. Coverage under a perfect selector is the fraction you would solve if you could see every correct draw.

Majority voting levels off after about 10 samples, or about 50. Coverage under a perfect selector keeps rising. The gap between those two curves is larger on MATH than on GSM8K. MATH is the harder set. On GSM8K many problems are simple enough that the right answer is common, so a vote works on many of them. Even there, the hardest items have a low count of correct answers. Reward models that score the finished answer still sit well below coverage. No newer gap size is claimed here.

On the hardest items a correct answer may appear once, twice, or three times in 1,000 or 10,000 samples. A majority cannot see it, because it is rare. Sets of 10,000 draws per problem were published so this gap can be studied.

PART E

Parallel samples, or a longer revision

The same token budget can buy many fresh starts, or a longer edit of one attempt. Easier questions and the hardest questions wanted different buys.

Parallel sampling starts over: several full answers to the same question. Sequential revision stays with one attempt, edits it, and answers when it is ready. An outcome reward model scores the finished answer. A process reward model scores each step. A step is a meaningful chunk, such as a line of a solution, not each token.

Parallel samples

Each draw starts fresh. Ten, a hundred, or a thousand separate answers. A vote, a reward model, or a domain checker then picks. This is the spend that coverage measures.

Sequential revision

The model writes a start, then revises it, looks from another angle, and continues until it is ready to answer. A prompt can ask for that. An instruction-tuned model can do it when asked. Reasoning models are trained so the revision shows up inside the trace.

Outcome scores and step scores

An outcome reward model looks at the finished answer and gives a score. Its quality is limited, especially on a new kind of task. A process reward model gives a score per step. Early process models were trained from human yes-or-no marks on those steps. You can train your own, or start from a process model already built for a nearby task.

Best-of-N with a verifier is the parallel case: draw n answers, score the outcomes, keep the best. A process score can also steer the drafting itself, which is the beam below. A beam: four samples, keep two

A simple mix draws two attempts, revises each one, then picks with an outcome score or a process score. PART F

Searching a pipeline under a call budget

Archon treats answer-time scaling as a search over a recipe of calls. The recipe has to live inside a budget.

A call budget is how many model calls you may spend. Fusion asks a model to read the question and K drafts, then write one new answer. An oracle picker knows which draft is best and can only return one of the drafts. It cannot blend them. itest is the search: inference-time architecture search.

The inputs are the benchmarks you care about, the call budget, the models you may call, and a menu of operators. The output is a pipeline. The search is Bayesian. It reached a strong pipeline after fewer configurations than greedy search or random selection. No count of those configurations is stated here. The final pipeline emits one response, so the score at the end is a pass@1 of the pipeline, not a coverage number.

The operators

These are prompting patterns. The models were not trained specially for this menu. A unit-test example: the task is balanced brackets. One generated test says a string with an odd number of brackets should be rejected. Another says a closing bracket must match the most recent open bracket that is still unmatched. The model can also be asked to judge a solution against the test in words. Running the test is the stronger check.

PART B

One score on the finished solution

The 2021 outcome verifier (Training verifiers to solve math problems) is a language model with a scalar head. The scalar is the probability that the solution is correct. It looks at the finished solution and ignores how the steps got there.

An outcome is the final answer. A scalar head is one extra number on top of the language model. Masked question tokens do not take part in the language-modeling loss. The model still reads them.

How that verifier was trained

Two losses share the run. A binary correctness loss teaches the scalar to match right or wrong. A language-modeling loss on the solution tokens keeps the verifier able to read a solution. Question tokens are masked out of that second loss. The generator is fine-tuned first, for two epochs. It then writes 100 completions per question, each labeled against the human final answer. The verifier trains for one epoch on those labels.

A judge that likes a certain shape of answer can be satisfied by skipping the work and jumping to a shape it likes. That is reward hacking: the steps disappear, and the text aims at the reward. PART A

Three sources, one job

Real tasks need an environment, a tool, or code, and a signal from that interaction. The three loops below differ in who is allowed to say the attempt was good.

Feedback here means a signal from outside the weights: a page, a test log, or a critique. Chain-of-thought is the model writing steps before the answer. Those steps can be fluent and still be unchecked. A policy is the model’s habit of what to write or which tool to call next.

Ask for today’s temperature and the weights have nothing current to read. A search is the step that fetches it. The same shape covers a purchase, a program, and a refusal: something outside the sentence has to answer.

PART B

Think, act, observe

People think, act, notice, and think again. A model that only writes steps has the first of those and misses the notice.

You are thirsty, so you walk to the kitchen. The water is out. The next plan starts from that fact. ReAct is that order for a model: a thought, then an allowed action, then the observation, then a new thought. The name is reasoning plus acting.

A thought stays in language. An action touches the world

Thought

The model writes a reason in the prompt. The page, the cart, and the test runner are unchanged. The tokens help because the model was trained on language, and the reason is a useful way to choose the next action. Thoughts are their own loop. They are not items in the action list.

Action

The model picks from a permitted list, the same kind of constraint a robot has when only some motor commands are legal. On the Wikipedia tasks the list is small: search, look up a string, or finish. The observation comes back into the prompt and is allowed to change the next thought.

The order is interleaved. Thought, action, thought, action. Each observation informs the next thought. A pile of thoughts written first, then a pile of actions, cannot see a page that has not been fetched. Parallel samples are possible, and they have to be designed on purpose. They are not the default schedule.

Chain-of-thought on “what is the temperature today?” can only remix the weights. Hallucination is the major failure of that pattern when the fact lives outside the model. A search supplies an external knowledge base, and the page sits in the trace where a person can read it.

What each baseline withholds

How this loop changed

  • Original. A frozen PaLM model, few-shot. The prompt holds example thoughts, actions, and observations. Valid actions are a closed list.
  • Next. Fine-tuning on the traces beats that prompt. An outer training loop beats the fine-tune. The traces also become something a later model can be distilled on, so the thought and the tool call show up inside ordinary reasoning.
  • Usual on 31 October 2026. Tool use is trained. The allowed-tool list is still the constraint. Search is the usual way in for a fact the weights do not hold. Written traces stay useful because a person can see which page the answer used.
  • Side paths. Action-only browser traces. Chain-of-thought with no tool. A compound system that sends subtasks to different models. Very long traces on easy questions: the cost is real, and there is no single usual cutoff for when to stop thinking. STILL OPEN
A large action list needs more demonstrations than a prompt can hold, which is why the few-shot form ran out of room. More reasoning steps also raise inference cost. The usual response is to train the loop, and to keep the tool list explicit, rather than to hope the model knows when it knows.
PART C

The test is the teacher

RLEF grounds code models in execution feedback. The action is a program. The observation is what the tests print. The reward is binary: pass or fail.

A public test is a small set the model is allowed to see fail, so it can try again. A private test is held back. It grades the attempt that will update the weights. PPO is the classical policy update that uses a learned value as a critic. You do not need its formula here.

Two clocks: try again, then update

At generation the model sees a natural-language problem and writes code. Public tests run. On a failure, the output is appended and the model tries again, until the public tests pass or a turn limit is hit. Solutions that pass the public set are then scored on a hidden private set, and that reward enters the policy update.

PART D

A constitution the model can apply

Helpful and harmless pull apart. Ranking every answer by hand does not scale. Constitutional AI writes the target down as principles and lets the model critique itself.

RLHF is reinforcement learning from human feedback: people compare answers, a reward model learns the comparisons, and a policy update hill-climbs that reward. RLAIF swaps in model feedback so the comparisons can scale. DPO is direct preference optimization: an offline update on stored pairs that skips the online reward-model loop. Elo here means a ranking from pairwise preferences. Higher means preferred more often. No Elo figures are used below. A Pareto frontier is the edge where raising one score costs the other.

The early loop: people rank pairs

Show two answers. Ask which is correct, which is useful, and which is specific enough. Train a reward model on those ranks. Hill-climb the policy. Tens of thousands of human labels make this slow. Filtering the pretraining text removes some material, and the open web is still in the weights, so the last-stage loop remains.

Post-training is the small last stage, described as on the order of 5% of the compute, and it is run again fairly often. A change of principles can live in that stage. That cost fact does not say the old rule has been forgotten.

People write the rules. The model does the marking

The original constitution is 16 human-written principles. A red-team prompt is a prompt built to tempt a bad answer. The model produces an output, a critique against a principle, and a revision. People are in the loop as authors of the principles, not as rankers of every output. The model has to notice the behavior and follow the rewrite instruction. That works because instruction following got good enough to carry a rule.

PART E

What stays open

The three loops assume a signal you can trust and a task whose steps you can name. Several neighboring questions are still open. The usual workaround is named so it is not mistaken for a solution.

The model is a weak judge of what it knows

Some accounts say the model knows when it knows. Ask it to rate its own answer and the usual result is overconfidence. Calibration is an open research problem. STILL OPEN You also do not want a search on “what is 1 + 1?”. The practical design is to require the tool where the answer depends on the world, and to keep a closed calculation on the path that does not search. That split is chosen by the application. It is not read off a confidence number.

A self-rated “sure” is a weak reason to skip the tool. The page is the check.

A bad observation becomes the next premise

Search results can contradict each other, and an environment can return something misleading. The next thought inherits that text. People reach for a second look, a backtrack when the chain is looping, another retrieval, or several samples and a vote. Those are add-ons. None of them is the settled repair. STILL OPEN A majority vote, already used as self-consistency, is one guardrail for a shaky answer. It does not make a noisy tool truthful.

1 · On HotpotQA and FEVER, how did ReAct compare with action-only prompting and with chain-of-thought?
ReAct beats action-only prompting. It beats chain-of-thought on FEVER. On HotpotQA it does not always beat chain-of-thought. Combining chain-of-thought self-consistency with a fallback to or from ReAct beats the standard techniques. The fallbacks are: after ReAct fails for a set number of steps, switch to chain-of-thought self-consistency; or, if the majority answer shows up less than half the time, switch to ReAct.
2 · In RLEF, what do the public tests do during generation, and what do the private tests do in the policy update?
Public tests are a small subset. Failure output is appended and the model tries again until the code passes or a turn limit is hit. Private tests stay hidden during generation. Their pass or fail is the reward that enters the update, so the model is not trained on outputs it was allowed to memorize. On the palindrome trace, turn 1 times out, the next try passes the public tests, and the private tests are what update the policy. The message is the test result, not a suggested fix.

Harmlessness rises. Helpfulness falls. The joint claim is that helpfulness and harmlessness together rise. Constitutional reinforcement learning with chain-of-thought is called the best Pareto frontier between helpfulness and harmlessness, ahead of constitutional supervised learning alone and ahead of the human-feedback RLHF comparisons. PART A

LATS: Monte Carlo tree search over language actions

The environment answers each action. The tree keeps several next actions alive until a value says which line was better.

Monte Carlo tree search picks a branch, plays it forward, and writes the result back up the path so the next pick is better informed. Here the “moves” are sentences and tool actions, and the environment is a page or a task, which is the feedback the tree reads.

What this search scores, beside a step reward

Session 03’s Math-Shepherd scores a step by whether fresh endings from that step still reach a known answer. LATS scores the outcomes of actions, then adds the model’s reflection. There is no proof the selection rule is optimal. Other bandit rules were not compared.

PART B

STaR: rationales from a check and a hint

Internet text rarely contains reasoning traces. STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner) starts from a small rationale set, generates the missing scratch work, and fine-tunes on what lands on the right answer.

A rationale is the scratch work before the answer. The big pool often has questions and answers only. STaR asks the model to write the scratch work, keeps the attempts whose final answer matches, and trains on those pairs. When the final answer is wrong, the correct answer is shown as a hint, the model writes a rationale anyway, and the hint is hidden again before training.

The loop, including the misses

  • Start small. On the order of 100 reasoning examples is enough to begin when there is no reinforcement-learning stack. The warm-up set has questions, rationales, and answers.
  • Generate wide. Solutions are generated for a large question set. 10k problems is a representative size. That large set has questions and answers only.
  • Keep the matches. Only correct final answers stay in the direct pool. Incorrect solutions are discarded from that pool.
  • Hint the rest. The answer is given, the model writes the rationale, and the hint is hidden at training time. The hint example is the answer 42. The hinted rationales are not filtered.
  • Fine-tune on both pools, then repeat. Successes and hinted rationales are both training data. Several iterations level off.

Worked item from the commonsense set. “Where do you put your grapes just before checking out?” The answer is B, grocery cart. The rationale is the sentence that was missing from the large set. Training sees the question, the rationale, and B. It does not see a hint that was used to draft the rationale.

Fine-tuning only on problems the model already gets right never shows it a harder one. The hint lets a miss become a training pair. Hiding the hint means the pair looks like a solve from the question alone.

PART C

GRPO: a group in place of a critic

DeepSeekMath is the 7B math result in the AIME table, and the top-1 result on the older Math benchmark. The algorithm that made the group update cheap is GRPO, group-relative policy optimization.

PPO (proximal policy optimization) was the classic update for reinforcement learning from human feedback. It trains the policy and a second network, the critic, that guesses how good the future looks. GRPO skips that second network. For each question it samples a group of answers, scores them, and treats “better than the others in this group” as the advantage. After DeepSeek-R1 (early 2025), that group comparison became the common update for verifiable-reward reasoning. PPO with a critic is still the older default for preference training, and both are in use.

Prime with code, then mine math from the web

  • Earlier attempt. Training on science and math papers, the arXiv pile, was the previous recipe for a math boost. It did not deliver the coverage.
  • What worked. Start from a code model, then curate math pages from Common Crawl. Code-to-math transferred. The web crawl, mined for coverage across math, yielded more useful tokens than the papers.
  • Usual on 31 Oct 2026. A reasoning run still starts from a strong base, often one that already codes, and then trains with a verifiable reward. Paper-only continued pre-training is the side path that lost on this comparison.

Four copies, and the critic GRPO drops

PPO keeps four copies in memory: the old policy, the new policy, the critic, and the reward model. That fits a 7B model and gets painful as you scale. GRPO drops the critic. The baseline is the group of samples for the same prompt, so the value network is gone.

An easy prompt hands out high scores because it is easy. A rare success on a hard prompt should count more. Other samples of the same prompt are the local comparison, and they replace a learned guess of “what this situation usually returns.”

The advantage is a comparison inside the group

For one question, sample a group. Score each member. The advantage of a member is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group’s standard deviation. The original PPO advantage came from a learned critic. This one comes from the siblings of the same prompt.

PART D

Long chains: controls that sit beside GRPO

Naive GRPO on Qwen-32B scores 30% on AIME. Entropy collapses, so the model gets too sure. Training is unstable. Length runs away. DAPO is the stack of controls that took that same run to 50%.

Entropy here is a stand-in for how much exploring the model can still do. Low entropy means the next-token distribution has collapsed onto a few choices. Clipping in PPO limits how far a token’s probability may move in one update. Symmetric clipping treats a rise and a fall alike, so a rare token can only climb a short way. Asymmetric clipping lets a rare token’s probability rise more, which is what keeps entropy from collapsing.
A plain group update on long answers. The model becomes over-confident, the run wobbles, and the answers get longer. A chain cut off by the length limit dumps noise into the gradient. A long wrong answer can weigh as much as a short right one when the loss is counted per sample.
